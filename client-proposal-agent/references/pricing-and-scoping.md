# Pricing and Scoping

The mechanics behind the three-option proposal. Read when the user asks how to price, what to include, or why the options are structured this way.

## Why three options

One price invites a yes/no. Three prices invite a choice, and choice moves the conversation from "is this worth it" to "which one fits". The middle option is chosen most often when it is clearly the best fit — so the middle must be the one you actually want to deliver.

The options are not good/better/best quality tiers. They are three honest ways to solve the same problem:

| Tier | What it optimises | Typical difference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Essential | The core outcome, standard pace | Smallest scope that still works |
| Recommended | The outcome plus the pieces that make it stick | Content, integration, or support added |
| Premium | Speed, risk removal, or strategic depth | Faster timeline, more senior time, ongoing help |

A deliberately broken Essential destroys trust when the client picks it. If Essential cannot solve the core problem, it is not an option — it is a different project.

## Setting the numbers

- **Start from the rate card.** Fixed price = estimated hours × rate + a risk buffer (15–30% for unclear briefs). Never invent a rate to hit a target.
- **Compute the effective rate.** Price ÷ realistic hours. If the Premium tier lands below the rate card, it is underpriced. If a tier is below your floor, cut scope instead.
- **Price the risk, not the word count.** A 6-page site with one integration can cost more than a 20-page brochure site.
- **Round to clean numbers.** $9,500 reads as a considered price; $9,473 reads like a spreadsheet leak.
- **Anchoring:** show Premium first in the table or the largest number first in the summary line, then Recommended. Most buyers read top-down.
- **Deposit:** 30–50% on signature is standard for new clients. Milestones for larger projects. Never 100% on delivery unless the client is established and trusted.
- **Discounts:** only with the user's explicit approval, and always trade something — reduced scope, longer timeline, case-study rights, or a testimonial. An untraded discount teaches the client the price was invented.

## Scoping traps

| Trap | What it costs | Guard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unlimited revisions | Weeks of unpaid work | Two rounds per deliverable, then hourly |
| "Just one more page" | Scope creep compounding | Page count and content responsibility defined |
| Content not supplied | Launch blocked, blame lands on you | Client responsibilities list, with dates |
| Integrations assumed | Unbounded technical risk | Name each integration; quote separately if uncertain |
| Multiple approvers | Endless feedback loops | One decision-maker named in the proposal |
| Training and handover assumed | Extra days | Included as a line item or explicitly excluded |
| Ongoing support assumed | Free retainer forever | Support is a separate monthly option |
| Rush timeline | Overtime or burnout | Rush fee or extended date, stated plainly |

## Discovery questions

Ask these before pricing if the brief is thin. Keep it to the ones that change the number:

1. What does success look like three months after launch, in measurable terms?
2. Who signs off, and who else has an opinion?
3. What exists today — brand, content, systems, accounts?
4. What is the hard deadline, and what happens if it slips?
5. What have you tried before, and why did it not work?
6. Is there a budget range already set?
7. Who maintains this after handover?

Answers 1, 4, and 7 move price the most.

## When to recommend a smaller scope

If the budget is below the honest cost of the ask, do not stretch the same scope thinner — it fails and damages the relationship. Propose a phased approach: solve the core problem now, defer the rest with a clear phase-two estimate. That is a stronger proposal than a discounted version of something you cannot deliver.

## Proposal length and follow-up

- 2–5 pages. Long enough to prove competence, short enough to be read on a phone.
- One decision per section; no section that only repeats another.
- Send with a one-paragraph email naming the recommended option and the next step.
- Follow up in three business days with one new piece of information — not "just checking in".
- Set a validity window (14–30 days) so pricing has an edge without fake urgency.
